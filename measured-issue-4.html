What lit up the market on Monday — and what it actually means for rates

Over the weekend of May 10–11, the U.S. and China announced a 90-day tariff truce. The deal brought tariffs on Chinese goods down from a staggering 145% — which had been pricing in near-total trade war conditions — to a more manageable 30%. Markets responded immediately, and not in the direction mortgage borrowers were hoping for.

Here's why good economic news sent rates higher — and this is important to understand. When investors feel better about the global economy, they move money out of safe-haven bonds and into riskier assets like stocks. Less demand for bonds means lower bond prices, which means higher bond yields. Higher 10-year Treasury yields mean higher mortgage rates. It's counterintuitive, but it's consistent.

📊 The Tariff-Rate Paradox · Mortgage News Daily / HousingWire

The China trade deal pushed rates to their highest levels in over two weeks on Monday morning. The move wasn't dramatic — rates climbed roughly 15–18 basis points intraday before settling — but the direction was clear. Tariffs create a push-pull on rates: lower tariffs reduce recession fears (bad for rates) but also reduce inflation fears (good for rates). Right now the recession-fear relief is winning.

Additionally, U.S. freight bookings out of China surged nearly 300% the week after the announcement as companies rushed to restock before the 90-day window closes. That surge in economic activity is also putting upward pressure on inflation expectations — and by extension, rates.

The Trump-Middle East summit also contributed to a more "risk-on" market mood this week, further pushing investors away from bonds. Add the CPI data that came in slightly warmer than expected on Monday morning and you have three forces all pointing the same direction: rates up.

The 30-year fixed rate climbed to its highest point in over two weeks immediately following the U.S.-China tariff pause announcement, as investors rotated out of bonds on improved economic confidence.

The honest assessment: rates are not going to free-fall anytime soon. The 90-day tariff window creates a period of artificial calm that prevents the kind of economic slowdown that would normally push the Fed toward cuts. Fannie Mae's latest forecast has the 30-year finishing Q2 at around 6.30%. NAHB still expects rates to dip just below 6% by year-end — but only if inflation cooperates. MBA forecasts put rates at 6.20–6.30% through most of 2026 with modest easing into 2027.

Will the market fully bounce back? The honest answer.

Every week I get some version of this question: "Is the housing market going to recover?" The answer depends entirely on what you mean by "recover."

If you mean back to 2021 — 3% rates, bidding wars on every listing, homes selling in 48 hours — that is not coming. Not this year, probably not ever. Those conditions were the product of historic Fed intervention during a global pandemic. They were an anomaly, not a baseline.

If you mean a functional, active market where buyers can buy and sellers can sell at fair prices — that market is already here, and it's improving steadily.

📈 Market Recovery Reality Check · Multiple Sources · May 2026

Home values are up 16% since 2022 despite rates tripling in that same period — which tells you how durable underlying housing demand is. New listings are up 9.7% year over year. Pending sales are rising. Refinance applications are up 62% year over year as homeowners who bought in 2023–2024 start finding windows to lower their rate. Affordability, measured by the MBA's Purchase Applications Payment Index, is 5.6% better than one year ago as wage growth catches up.

The market isn't broken. It's recalibrating. And the buyers who move during recalibration consistently outperform the ones who wait for the all-clear — because by the time the all-clear sounds, the prices have already re-priced upward to reflect improved confidence.

Zillow projects home values to rise 1.2% nationally in 2026 — modest, stable appreciation rather than the chaos of 2021 or the stagnation of 2023. That's a healthy market. The buyers who enter it this spring are buying into stability, not a peak and not a bottom. That's actually the best place to buy.

UWM LIVE! — what I saw, and what it means for your loan right now

I spent this week at UWM LIVE! in Pontiac, Michigan — United Wholesale Mortgage's annual broker event. If you've never heard of it, think of it as the moment each year where the largest wholesale lender in the country shows brokers what they've built and what's coming. This year it was significant.

Two announcements stood out. One has an immediate dollar impact on anyone thinking about refinancing. The other changes how brokers can serve clients at scale. Both are worth knowing about.

🏦 Refi '86 — The Most Aggressive Refi Incentive UWM Has Offered This Year

At UWM LIVE!, Mat Ishbia announced Refi '86 — an 86-basis-point pricing incentive on refinances, available through June 30. The name pays homage to UWM's founding in 1986 and what Ishbia called "four decades of unparalleled trust and partnership" with the broker community.

Here's what 86 basis points means in plain terms: on a refinance where the wholesale rate is already 0.375–0.50% below retail, this incentive stacks an additional 0.86% pricing advantage on top of that. The practical effect is that borrowers who refinanced in 2023 or 2024 at rates in the high 6s or 7s could be looking at meaningful monthly savings right now — not someday, right now.

Refi '86 applies to conventional and government refinance loans using PA+ or TRAC+/TRAC Lite with a 680 or higher FICO score and runs alongside UWM's existing free 1-0 temporary rate buydown offer on purchases. Both programs run through June 30. After that, they're gone.

🤖 Mia — UWM's AI Loan Officer Assistant Just Got More Powerful

Last year at UWM LIVE!, UWM debuted Mia — an AI voice assistant built specifically for mortgage brokers. The idea: handle the administrative workload so loan officers can focus on the work that actually requires a human. In the year since launch, Mia has helped close 80,000 loans.

This year's upgrades include Mia On Demand — giving brokers direct control over when and how Mia contacts clients — plus Mia Español, expanding access to Spanish-speaking borrowers. UWM CEO Mat Ishbia has been direct about the philosophy: he wants brokers focused on "$500 work, not $15 work." The high-value conversations with clients. The strategy. The relationships. Let Mia handle the scheduling, follow-ups, and routine information.

UWM's in-house AI has also helped underwriters increase their processing capacity from 6 loans per day to 16 — a result of the Google Cloud Gemini integration announced earlier this year. Faster underwriting means faster closings. For borrowers, that's real.

The broader picture from UWM LIVE! was a company firing on all cylinders. UWM posted $44.9 billion in Q1 2026 originations — up 39% year over year and their second-best first quarter ever — with $170.4 million in net income reversing a $247 million loss from Q1 2025. The broker channel is winning. The question is whether the broker you're working with is set up to take full advantage of it.

What to watch next week

The 90-day tariff clock is now ticking. The question markets are going to spend the next three months trying to answer is: does this truce become a real deal, or does it expire and escalate? The answer will do more to shape the second half of 2026 mortgage rates than anything the Fed says.

Watch oil: WTI crude is back above $90 on improved economic sentiment. If it climbs toward $100, inflation expectations follow and rates go with them. If the Iran situation stabilizes and oil pulls back, that's the tailwind that could get the 30-year back toward 6.0%.

Watch the 10-year Treasury: It's been trading between 4.30–4.45% this week. A sustained break above 4.50% pushes mortgage rates toward 6.60–6.75%. A pullback below 4.25% gets us back toward the low 6s. This is the single number that matters most.

Watch housing starts: Single-family starts dropped 12% in April year over year — a direct result of tariff uncertainty on building materials. If that number doesn't recover, supply stays constrained and prices stay elevated even if demand softens. More evidence that waiting for a price crash is a losing strategy in most markets.
