The reason the 10-year has been under pressure all month — and why any improvement matters — comes back to two things: oil prices and inflation data.

The Iran war drove gas to $4.54 per gallon at its Memorial Day peak — the highest price at the pump since July 2022. Since the war started in late February, gas has risen more than $1.60 per gallon, a 54% spike. Higher energy costs feed directly into inflation expectations, which push bond investors to demand higher yields, which pushes mortgage rates up. The chain is that direct.

But here's where it gets interesting. This week, reports began circulating that the U.S. and Iran may be close to a peace deal. Oil dropped below $100 a barrel on the news. Wholesale gas prices fell 5% in a single session. Treasury yields dipped. And according to Mortgage News Daily, the average 30-year rate intraday fell to 6.38% on the day the news broke — its lowest reading in weeks — before partially retracing.

If a confirmed peace deal materializes and oil prices sustain a move lower — WTI below $88 is the threshold that historically feeds through to meaningful mortgage rate relief — the 10-year Treasury has room to fall back toward 4.20–4.30%. At that level, the 30-year mortgage tracks toward the low-to-mid 6% range, potentially shedding 20–30 basis points from current levels within two weeks of confirmation.

That's not a prediction. That's what the math showed on a single afternoon of deal speculation this week. The demand is already waiting — pending home sales have risen three months in a row per Freddie Mac's chief economist. The buyers are there. They're just waiting for a rate that makes the monthly payment work.

On the inflation side: April's PCE — the Fed's preferred inflation gauge — came in at 3.80% year-over-year for headline and 3.30% for core. Both are elevated above the Fed's 2% target, which is exactly why the Fed held rates at its last meeting and why markets are pricing zero probability of a June cut. The next PCE reading, covering May data, releases in late June — and that will be the first chance to see whether the energy-driven inflation spike is starting to fade.

Thinking about buying a condo? Read this before you fall in love with one.

Condos are one of the most misunderstood purchases in the mortgage world. Buyers fall in love with a unit, get pre-approved based on their credit and income, go under contract — and then find out ten days before closing that the building doesn't qualify for the financing they planned on. It's one of the most preventable surprises in real estate and it happens constantly.

Here's what most buyers don't know: when you finance a condo, your lender is not just evaluating you. They're evaluating the entire building and the homeowners association that runs it. A borrower with an 800 credit score and a clean DTI can get denied — not because of anything they did, but because the building failed the review.

🏢 Warrantable vs. Non-Warrantable — The Distinction That Changes Everything

A warrantable condo meets Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac's guidelines. That means standard conventional financing — normal rates, normal down payments, normal underwriting. You're in the mainstream market.

A non-warrantable condo falls outside those guidelines. You can still buy it, but your financing options narrow dramatically. You're looking at portfolio loans, rates typically 0.50–1.00% above conventional, down payment requirements often at 20–30%, and far fewer lenders willing to touch the deal at all.

The gap between warrantable and non-warrantable can be the difference between a 6.50% rate and a 7.50% rate on the exact same unit in the exact same building — based entirely on how the HOA has been managed, not on anything the buyer controls.

This got significantly more important in March 2026. Fannie Mae issued Lender Letter LL-2026-03, which eliminated the "Limited Review" option for condos, raised minimum HOA reserve requirements to 15% of annual budget, capped master policy insurance deductibles at $50,000, and eliminated Florida's PERS (Project Eligibility Review Service). That last one hit Florida condo buyers especially hard, where coastal buildings and aging structures were already under scrutiny post-Surfside.

What commonly makes a condo non-warrantable right now:

✗
A single entity owns more than 25% of units — common in newer buildings where the developer still holds inventory
✗
More than 15% of HOA dues are 30+ days delinquent — signals financial distress in the association
✗
HOA reserves below 15% of annual budget — new 2026 Fannie Mae requirement; many older buildings now failing this threshold for the first time
✗
Active litigation involving the HOA — almost always disqualifies conventional financing regardless of the merits of the case
✗
Less than 51% owner-occupied — high investor concentration raises agency risk flags
📋 What to Do Before You Write an Offer on a Condo

Step 1: Request the HOA financials and condo questionnaire before going under contract. A good broker can review these and give you a warrantability read within 24–48 hours. If the seller won't provide them, that tells you something.

Step 2: Check the Fannie Mae and FHA approved condo lists. These are public. If the building is on them, you're in strong shape. If not, you need to know before you're emotionally committed.

Step 3: Work with a broker, not a retail bank. Banks have rigid internal guidelines and limited options when a building falls outside the box. A wholesale broker with access to multiple lenders — including portfolio lenders who specialize in non-warrantable deals — can often find a path the bank can't. And when the building is warrantable, you're already getting a better rate to start with.

The opportunity in condos right now is genuine — they're 54% more affordable than single-family homes in the 25 largest metro areas per Zillow data, and inventory is improving. The financing complexity is just as real. Don't let the second one blindside you after the first one has you excited.

📍 From Antigua — On Rest, Marriage, and Why It Matters

I've been thinking this week about what it actually means to do this job well over the long run. And one of the things I've learned — the hard way — is that the best version of me as an advisor shows up when I've protected time away from the desk.

Mortgage is an intense business. The phone doesn't stop. Rates move every day. Clients are navigating the biggest financial decisions of their lives and they need someone sharp on the other end of the call. You cannot be that person running on empty. Rest isn't a luxury in this business. It's part of the job, done right.

And more importantly — Cameron comes first. Not the rate sheet, not the pipeline, not the next deal. My marriage is the most important partnership I have. The trips, the intentional time away, the slow mornings where the phone stays face-down — those aren't breaks from what matters. They are what matters. Everything else is supposed to be in service of building a life worth living, not just a pipeline worth managing.

I say this because I think a lot of people reading Measured are in similar seasons. Busy careers. Building families. The mortgage industry has a culture of always-on that I genuinely believe does more harm than good over time. The advisors I respect most — the ones with decades of staying power and client relationships that span generations — all have this in common: they protect their time off with the same seriousness they protect their clients' financial interests.

So if you've sent me something this week and I haven't responded yet — it's coming Monday morning. And if you've been putting off a real trip or a weekend with your phone face-down: take it from someone writing a newsletter from the Caribbean on his birthday. Your clients will understand. The market will still be here.

What to watch heading into June

June is setting up to be a pivotal month. Here's what I'll be watching closely.

📅 June Market Signals — What Matters Most

Iran Peace Deal Progress — ongoing. The single most important non-economic variable for mortgage rates right now. Every credible step toward a ceasefire drops oil prices and bond yields with it. WTI crude below $88 is the threshold where rate relief becomes meaningful for borrowers. Watch oil as your real-time leading indicator — it moves before the Freddie Mac survey does.

CPI for May — Wednesday, June 10. April CPI came in at 3.8% — the highest since May 2023, driven almost entirely by energy. If May's reading starts to moderate as oil prices ease, bond markets will take notice quickly. A reading at or below 3.5% would be a positive signal for rates.

Fed Meeting — June 17–18. Kevin Warsh chairs his first FOMC meeting as the new Fed Chair. No rate cut is expected — markets are pricing zero probability of a June move. But the statement language and Warsh's first press conference will signal how the new chair thinks about the inflation vs. growth tradeoff. The bond market will react to tone as much as substance.

PCE for May — late June. April's PCE came in at 3.80% headline and 3.30% core — both well above the Fed's 2% target. The May reading, released in late June, will be the first look at whether the energy-driven inflation spike is beginning to fade. A meaningful drop toward 3.0% or below sets the table for rate relief in Q3.

UWM Refi '86 — expires June 30. The 86-basis-point wholesale refinance incentive ends at the end of the month. If you're at 7.0% or above and haven't run the numbers, June is the window. After June 30 this pricing goes away.

The setup for the second half of 2026 is more constructive than the 6.53% headline suggests. A peace deal, lower oil, and moderating inflation are three forces that — if they align — could bring the 30-year back to the low 6% range before Labor Day. That's not guaranteed. But it's a real scenario, and the borrowers who are positioned and ready when it happens will be glad they didn't wait for the all-clear that never comes with a flashing neon sign.
