The Strait of Hormuz — why a signed deal doesn't mean the crisis is over

This is the most important section of this issue. Most people read the headline — "peace deal signed at Versailles" — and assume the oil shock is over. It isn't. And understanding why matters enormously for where rates go from here.

The Strait of Hormuz is a 21-mile-wide waterway between Iran and Oman. Before February 28, 2026, 20% of the world's crude oil and 20% of global liquefied natural gas passed through it every day. It is the most consequential energy chokepoint in the world. When it was closed, the International Energy Agency called it the "largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market" — larger than the 1970s oil shock, larger than the Gulf War.

⚠️ Why the Strait Could Close Again — And What It Would Mean

The deal signed Tuesday is a memorandum of understanding — not a final treaty. The history of this conflict includes a ceasefire on April 8 that collapsed the same day. Three negotiation attempts failed before Tuesday's signing. The IRGC has been "less volatile" since the deal, not absent. Mines remain in the water and experts say clearing them could take six months.

Iran also laid down a condition that shipping industry group BIMCO called deeply problematic: the IRGC is charging a toll of approximately $1 per barrel — roughly $2 million per very large crude carrier transit. This gives the Revolutionary Guard a revenue stream from the Strait even under a "peace" framework, and BIMCO warned it could set a global precedent for other chokepoints.

The bigger risk: Kpler estimates that even with the deal in place, clearing mines takes 6 months, ships leaving and reloading takes 2–3 months, and restarting shut-in oil production in some countries — particularly Iraq — could take up to a year. Saudi Arabia and UAE can move faster through alternate pipelines. But the full flow of pre-war oil supply returning to the market is not a 2026 event under most scenarios.

If the deal collapses — for any reason, including resumed Israeli strikes, Iranian hardliner resistance, or a failed ratification process — the Strait closes again, oil prices re-spike toward $100+, inflation expectations surge, bond yields climb, and mortgage rates follow within days. Wall Street analysts had been modeling a potential $200/barrel scenario if the closure continued indefinitely. That tail risk has not gone to zero.

📉 The Mortgage Rate Math If the Deal Holds

Before the war, oil was trading around $68–72 per barrel. The Strait closure sent it surging. Goldman Sachs estimates a durable reopening brings WTI crude back toward $70–75 within 30 days as supply returns to market. At that level, U.S. inflation expectations fall meaningfully — potentially pulling the 10-year Treasury back toward 4.10–4.20%.

Apply the current 205-basis-point spread between the 10-year and the 30-year mortgage — which we covered in depth in Issue 8 — and a 4.15% 10-year implies a 30-year mortgage at approximately 6.20%. If spreads also continue to normalize toward the historical 170-basis-point average, you're looking at a 30-year closer to 5.85–6.00% by late summer. That's the optimistic scenario. It requires the deal to hold, oil to fall, and inflation to cooperate. None of those are guaranteed — but all of them are possible.

The shipping industry's own language this week captures the uncertainty precisely. BIMCO, the global shipping trade group: "Due to lack of details and a history of overly optimistic reassurances, we believe the security situation for the shipping industry remains volatile." The threat of attack remains "a strong possibility" even as the IRGC has become less active. Mine-free routes still need to be established. And Iran has demanded the right to collect tolls — a condition that undermines the principle of free navigation that the U.S. Navy has protected for decades.

The bottom line: the deal is real. The relief is real. The risk that it unravels is also real. This is not the end of the story — it is the beginning of what may be a long, uncertain reopening process.

HousingWire's Logan Mohtashami — whose Housing Market Tracker I reference weekly — has a rule of thumb I want to make sure every Measured reader knows: housing data tends to soften noticeably when the 30-year mortgage rate is above 6.64%, and especially when it breaks above 7%. We've stayed below 6.64% for most of 2026. That threshold is the line between a market that grinds and a market that moves. Right now at 6.47% we're comfortably below it.

Mohtashami's assessment of the first half, published this week, is worth quoting directly: "Housing has weathered the 2026 storm of crazy headlines and inflation as well as it possibly can." That's the data talking. Not optimism — actual weekly pending sales running ahead of last year despite the most disruptive geopolitical and inflationary shock since the 1970s.

That's the demand that's sitting on the sideline right now. When rates return to that level — and the Strait reopening scenario gets us there — that demand doesn't trickle back. It floods back.

Anthony's Take — The Second Half Setup

Here's the scenario I'm watching for: the Iran deal holds, mines get cleared over the next 60–90 days, oil falls toward $75, the 10-year Treasury drops to 4.10–4.20%, and mortgage rates drift toward 6.00–6.20%. At that level, 237,000 sidelined buyers come back to a market that doesn't have enough inventory to absorb them cleanly. Prices firm. Multiple offers return in competitive markets. The buyers who moved when it was quiet — before that moment — will be glad they didn't wait for the all-clear.

I'm not calling the bottom. I don't know exactly when the Strait fully clears or whether the deal survives its first stress test. What I do know is that the setup for the second half of 2026 is the most constructive it's been all year. And the borrowers who understand the difference between a 6.47% retail rate and what they'd actually qualify for through the wholesale channel are already ahead of the ones reading the CNN headline and waiting.
