At 2:00 PM ET today, Kevin Warsh chaired his first Federal Reserve meeting. The committee voted 12-0 to hold the federal funds rate at 3.50–3.75%. No surprise there. But everything around that decision — the dot plot, the statement, the inflation projections — told a different story than markets were hoping for.

Warsh came in and immediately made his presence known. The post-meeting statement was cut to 130 words — Warsh has long criticized the Fed for over-communicating, and he acted on it immediately. Gone was all language suggesting future rate cuts. Gone was any easing bias. What remains is a statement so stripped-down it tells you almost nothing — which is itself a message.

The dot plot was the real headline. Nine of eighteen FOMC members now project at least one rate hike before the end of 2026. Six of those nine want two hikes. The committee that entered 2026 projecting cuts is now majority-leaning toward tightening. The reason: inflation is at a three-year high, driven almost entirely by energy prices from the Iran war.

⚠️ What This Means for Your Mortgage Rate

The Fed doesn't set mortgage rates — the 10-year Treasury does. But a committee that's leaning toward hikes sends inflation expectations higher, which pushes Treasury yields up, which pushes your rate up. The current 30-year sits at approximately 6.52%. A 25-basis-point hike — if it materializes — would likely push that toward 6.70–6.77% within days of the announcement. On a $400,000 loan that's an additional $45–60 per month.

The Fed projects PCE inflation at 3.6% by year-end — nearly double their 2% target. Until that number comes down, every meeting carries hike risk. The days of waiting for the Fed to rescue your rate are over for 2026.

Warsh has criticized the Fed for overcommunicating. Today's 130-word communiqué — compared to 341 words in April — was his opening statement. Less guidance. More uncertainty. And uncertainty in bond markets almost always widens spreads and lifts rates.

🕊️ The One Thing Working In Your Favor Right Now

On Monday June 16 — the day before today's Fed meeting — President Trump announced the U.S. and Iran had reached a peace agreement restoring access through the Strait of Hormuz. The 10-year Treasury yield dropped to 4.46% on the news. Oil pulled back. Inflation expectations softened.

That move is the counterweight to today's hawkish Fed signal. If the Iran deal holds and oil falls toward $75–80 per barrel, the inflation picture improves quickly — and the case for a Fed hike weakens with it. The hike dots in today's plot assume oil stays elevated. If it doesn't, those dots get revised down in September.

Watch WTI crude daily. It is the most important variable for your mortgage rate right now — more than anything the Fed says.

The Bottom Line

Today the Fed held rates and told you the next move might be up. Warsh stripped the statement bare. Nine members want to hike. Inflation is running hot. This is not an environment where you sit back and wait for rates to fall on their own.

The Iran peace deal is real and its impact on oil — and therefore inflation and rates — could be significant. The two forces are pulling in opposite directions right now. The borrowers who win in this environment are the ones already positioned, not the ones scrambling after the signal becomes obvious.
