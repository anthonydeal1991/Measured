-23,000 jobs this morning — the first outright loss in months

The Bureau of Labor Statistics released the July employment report at 8:30am this morning and the headline stopped people mid-sentence: nonfarm payrolls fell by 23,000 in July. Wall Street had expected a gain of 83,000. That's a miss of more than 100,000 jobs. It is the first outright monthly job loss since the labor market stalled in 2025 and the most significant labor market print of 2026 by a wide margin.

The details make the headline worse. Government jobs fell by 53,000 — led by a loss of 50,000 in local government education. Retail trade shed 19,000. The unemployment rate dipped to 4.1% from 4.2%, but the improvement came almost entirely from workers leaving the labor force — the labor force participation rate fell to 61.4%, its lowest level in over five years. Average hourly earnings rose just 2 cents, pushing the 12-month wage growth rate down to 3.2% — the lowest since May 2021.

May and June were also revised sharply lower — a combined 103,000 fewer jobs than originally reported. The three-month average is now just 20,000 — a level consistent with a labor market that is stalling, not merely cooling. This is not a single bad month. It is the clearest signal yet that the Fed's higher-for-longer stance is extracting real cost from the labor market.

📊 What This Does to the Rate Picture — Immediately

The jobs miss matters enormously for mortgage rates. Here's the transmission mechanism: weak jobs → reduced rate hike probability → bond markets rally → Treasury yields fall → mortgage rates improve. September rate hike probability, which had climbed back toward 60% on recent inflation data, will fall sharply in the coming days as the market absorbs this print.

The 10-year Treasury opened Friday at 4.647% before easing slightly to 4.639% as the session progressed. The market's first reaction to the jobs miss was muted — oil rising to $77.11 and gold at $4,376 kept inflation expectations elevated even as labor data softened. But the full repricing of September FOMC expectations will play out over the coming week. Citi's economics team — out-of-consensus for months — now has a well-founded basis for their call: three rate cuts between now and January 2027.

The FOMC voted 9-3 to hold at last week's July 28-29 meeting. Three members wanted to cut. Before this morning's report that was a minority view. After it, the balance of risks has visibly shifted.

The asset depletion loan — what it is, how it works, and why your FA needs to know about it

This section is for a specific borrower that most loan officers quietly struggle to help — and most financial advisors don't realize a solution exists. The borrower has significant investable assets. A well-managed portfolio. Potentially a business, real estate holdings, and real wealth by every observable measure. But their documented income on paper — especially if they're retired, running a business in a growth phase, or drawing minimally from their portfolio — doesn't tell the underwriter what their balance sheet tells everyone else. They are asset-rich and income-qualification-challenged. And they've been underserved by conventional lending for years.

The product that changes this is called an asset depletion loan — also known as an asset qualifier or asset dissipation mortgage. It is a Non-QM loan that converts verified liquid assets into qualifying income, without requiring W-2s, tax returns, or proof of employment. The math is straightforward and, for the right borrower, extraordinarily powerful.

📐 How the Math Works — The 60-Month Divisor

The most aggressive Non-QM lenders use a 60-month divisor — meaning your eligible liquid assets are divided by 60 months to generate a monthly qualifying income figure. This is the number that goes into the debt-to-income calculation. It's not money paid to the borrower. It's the income figure the underwriter uses to determine what loan the borrower can support.

Compare that to agency programs (Fannie Mae / Freddie Mac), which divide by 360 months — producing one-sixth of the income and dramatically limiting loan size. The 60-month divisor is what makes Non-QM asset depletion work for high-net-worth borrowers with real purchasing power. Same assets, six times the qualifying income.

🔢 Asset Depletion — Working Example
Total liquid assets (brokerage + cash) $1,500,000
Less: down payment + closing costs − $250,000
Eligible assets remaining $1,250,000
÷ 60 months (Non-QM divisor) = $20,833 / mo qualifying income
Supports loan of approximately ~$1,000,000 – $1,200,000

The same $1,500,000 in assets under a conventional agency program — dividing by 360 — produces just $3,472/month in qualifying income. That number won't get a meaningful loan done. The 60-month Non-QM program produces $20,833/month and closes deals that conventional lending simply cannot touch.

💡 Why This Product Is Built for FA Clients Specifically

Financial advisors work with exactly the clients this loan was designed for. The retired executive with $2M under management and a portfolio income that doesn't look like a W-2. The business owner taking minimal salary in a growth year. The inherited wealth client who has never drawn meaningfully on their assets and whose tax returns look like a puzzle. These clients want to buy homes — sometimes significant homes — and have consistently been told conventional lending can't help them.

Here's the strategic layer that makes this conversation even more valuable for the FA: the asset depletion loan allows the client to keep their assets invested and growing rather than liquidating to buy the home. A client with $2M in a well-managed portfolio facing a $500,000 purchase doesn't need to pull half their portfolio out of the market. They use the asset depletion loan, put down 20%, finance the rest using the portfolio as the income qualifier, and keep the remaining assets working. The FA keeps the AUM. The client keeps the growth. The mortgage gets done.

The rate trade-off is real: Non-QM asset depletion loans typically price 50–150 basis points above conventional — at today's market, that means rates in the high 6s to mid 7s depending on FICO and LTV. But for a borrower choosing between liquidating $500,000 from a portfolio generating 8–10% annually and paying a 7.25% mortgage rate, the math heavily favors keeping the assets invested. That's the conversation the FA and the mortgage advisor need to have together.

📋 What Qualifies — and What Doesn't

Eligible assets (counted at or near 100%): Cash, checking, savings, CDs, money market funds. These are fully liquid with no volatility discount.

Eligible assets with haircuts: Stocks, bonds, mutual funds, ETFs — typically counted at 70–80% of market value to buffer against volatility. Brokerage accounts generally qualify at this level.

Retirement accounts: Counted at 60–70% if the borrower is under retirement age (the penalty-withdrawal discount). Rises to 80–90%+ once penalty-free withdrawals are available. The specific percentage varies by lender — always confirm.

What does NOT qualify: Real estate equity (not liquid), business ownership interests (not verifiable as liquid monthly income), unvested stock options. These are wealth, but they cannot be divided and deployed monthly in the underwriting model. For real estate equity specifically, a cash-out refi or DSCR product is the right path.

Minimum requirements (Non-QM 60-month programs): Typically 700 FICO minimum, 20% down payment, post-closing asset reserves of the greater of $500,000 or the loan amount plus 60 months of debt service. These are real requirements — this is not a low-documentation product for under-qualified borrowers. It is a different documentation method for correctly qualified ones.

Anthony's Take — Why I Want Every FA I Work With to Know This Exists

I've had the conversation too many times: a financial advisor refers a client, the client has $1.5 million under management, they want to buy a $900,000 home, and the income picture doesn't support a conventional loan. Most of those conversations used to end at "I'll refer you to a private bank" or "you'll need to liquidate some assets." Neither answer serves the client well.

The asset depletion loan changes that outcome. It is a legitimate, well-structured Non-QM product with real underwriting discipline — not a workaround. The higher rate is a real trade-off. But for the right client — asset-rich, income-qualified by balance sheet rather than pay stub — it's the conversation that closes the deal and preserves the relationship on both sides. If you're a financial advisor reading this and you've had a client in this situation, reach out. I'd rather talk through the scenario before they give up than after.

Stop playing middle man — why the call with the buyer's agent changes everything

There's a version of this job that most loan officers default to: receive the application, send the pre-approval, answer the borrower's questions, and let the real estate agent and borrower manage the offer strategy between themselves. The LO shows up when documents are needed and disappears in between.

That version of the job leaves deals on the table. It leaves clients underserved. And it leaves real estate agents without a partner they can actually count on — which means they send the next deal to someone else.

📞 What Actually Happens When You Get on the Phone with the Agent

The buyer's agent knows things the borrower often doesn't. They know what the seller's situation is, what concessions are realistic, whether a quick close is more valuable than a higher price in this particular deal, what the appraisal risk looks like on the property, and whether competing offers are likely. That context is the difference between a pre-approval letter and a winning offer strategy.

When you pick up the phone and call the agent directly — not as an order-taker, but as a collaborator — the conversation changes. You find out the seller needs 45 days but your borrower can close in 21. You find out there's a competing cash offer and you need to structure a bridge scenario or escalation clause. You find out the asking price is $30,000 above the likely appraisal and the borrower needs to be prepared for that gap.

None of that information arrives in the loan application. All of it affects the outcome of the transaction. The loan officers who make that call are the ones agents remember. The ones who don't are the ones agents swap out on the next deal.

It's also a different conversation with the agent than the borrower's version of events. Borrowers sometimes filter what they tell the lender. Agents almost never do — they need the deal to close and they need a lender who understands the full picture. When you call the agent, you learn the real story. That's the call that gets done right.

The practical rule: on every purchase transaction, call the buyer's agent before the pre-approval letter goes out. Learn the property, the seller's situation, and the competitive landscape. Then structure the pre-approval — and the rate lock strategy — around what you actually know, not what the borrower guessed. That call takes ten minutes and changes the probability of closing meaningfully. Almost no one does it consistently. That is exactly why it's the advantage.

The willingness to fail — why the risk of a no is where all the growth is

Something I've been thinking about more as the referral business grows: the gap between advisors who have comfortable, predictable pipelines and advisors who build genuinely expanding businesses is almost never skill. It's almost always willingness to put themselves in situations where the answer might be no.

It's easy to work with agents you already have relationships with. The repeat calls, the reliable closings, the comfortable dynamic — none of that requires risk. But the agent sending you three deals a month was a stranger once. Somewhere in that history is a first call you made, a first meeting you asked for, a first deal you probably had to earn. That moment was uncomfortable. The outcome was not guaranteed.

On Compounding Failure Into Success

The advisors I've watched build the most durable businesses are not the ones who failed the least. They're the ones who failed the most — and kept going anyway. They asked agents for business who said no. They followed up on referrals that went cold. They showed up at events where they didn't know anyone and introduced themselves to rooms full of people who had no reason to care yet.

Each no is a data point. Each awkward conversation is practice. Each relationship that doesn't go anywhere is still a person who knows your name and might remember it when something changes. The willingness to fail is not just a character trait. It's a compounding asset. The more rejection you absorb without pulling back, the more surface area you have for success to land on.

The comfortable version of this business — the one that depends entirely on the relationships you already have — is fragile. Agents retire. Clients move. Markets shift. The advisor who has only ever worked with people they already know has a pipeline that is one life change away from stalling. The advisor who has consistently put themselves in new rooms, made new calls, and accepted the discomfort of asking — that business compounds in a way the comfortable one never does.

The goal is not to be unafraid of failure. The goal is to do the thing anyway — and to let the accumulated attempts do the work that talent alone never will.

Practically: pick one agent this week you don't currently work with but respect. Make the call. Not to pitch. To learn. What are they seeing in their market? What do their clients struggle with most? What does their ideal lending partner look like? Listen for ten minutes. The answer to that question is a roadmap for becoming exactly what they need — and that is how referral relationships actually begin.
